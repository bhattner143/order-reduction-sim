\section{The story in one page}
\label{sec:story}
% =============================================================================

You hold a cup of coffee and learn to move it without spilling. The cup has its
own physics (weight, wobble, slosh). Your arm has physics too (stiffness,
damping). Together they form one \textbf{coupled system}.

Thrishanth Nanayakkara's idea, developed after the July 2026 meeting, is:

\begin{quote}
When the coupled system is \textbf{high order} (many ways to wobble), the brain
may \textbf{stiffen the arm} first. Stiffening pushes the fast wobbles so far
into the past that, during one reach, you mostly feel the \textbf{slow,
dominant} response. You learn that slow part safely. Then you \textbf{relax}
and learn the fast parts on top.
\end{quote}

The simulation in \texttt{order-reduction-sim} tests this idea with two
different computer learners. Both use the same cup and the same five tuning
schedules; they differ in \emph{how} they learn.

\begin{examplebox}{Cup of coffee}
\textbf{Soft arm, fast reach:} the coffee sloshes, the cup wobbles, you feel
many things at once. If your mental model is wrong, the movement fails (spill).

\textbf{Stiff arm, slow reach:} you squeeze hard. Slosh dies in milliseconds.
During your 0.8\,s reach you mostly feel ``how heavy/sluggish is this cup?''
--- one main lag. Safer, simpler, but you cannot yet learn the slosh.

\textbf{Then relax:} once you know the main lag, you soften the arm and move
faster. The slosh reappears in what you feel. Now you can learn it --- but only
if your coarse model is already good enough that the reach does not fail.
\end{examplebox}

\section{Part I --- The second-order mass--spring--damper}
\label{sec:msd}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{The physical equation}

Along one movement axis, the coupled arm and object are modelled as a
\textbf{mass} pulled by a \textbf{spring} and slowed by a \textbf{dashpot}
(damper):
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\; M\ddot x + B\dot x + K x = F(t)\;}
\label{eq:msd}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item $M\ddot x$ --- inertia resists \emph{acceleration} (``hard to get moving'').
\item $B\dot x$ --- damping resists \emph{velocity} (``motion is slowed, wobbles die'').
\item $Kx$ --- stiffness pulls back toward rest (``rubber band'').
\item $F(t)$ --- your muscle force.
\end{itemize}

\begin{examplebox}{Mass--spring--damper}
Push a shopping trolley. $M$ is the trolley mass. $K$ is how stiffly you
hold the handle (and any elastic in the object). $B$ is friction in the wheels
and how much you resist the handle moving. Co-contraction in the arm increases
both $B$ and $K$ --- you grip tighter and resist motion more.
\end{examplebox}

\subsection{Laplace transform and transfer function}

Take the Laplace transform of \eqref{eq:msd} (zero initial conditions):
\[
Ms^2 X(s) + Bs X(s) + K X(s) = F(s)
\]
Solve for the ratio $X(s)/F(s)$:
\begin{equation}
G(s) = \frac{X(s)}{F(s)} = \frac{1}{Ms^2 + Bs + K}
\label{eq:secondorderG}
\end{equation}
This $G(s)$ is the \textbf{transfer function}: it tells you how each frequency
in $F$ is amplified or delayed in $x$.

\subsection{Natural frequency and damping ratio}

Define:
\begin{align}
\omega_n &= \sqrt{\frac{K}{M}} \quad &&\text{(natural frequency, rad/s)} \label{eq:omegan}\\
\xi &= \frac{B}{2\sqrt{MK}} \quad &&\text{(damping ratio, dimensionless)} \label{eq:xi}
\end{align}

\begin{examplebox}{What $\xi$ means}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item $\xi<1$: underdamped --- oscillates before settling (bouncy).
\item $\xi=1$: critically damped --- returns to rest as fast as possible without overshoot.
\item $\xi>1$: overdamped --- no oscillation; two real exponential decays stacked.
\end{itemize}
Co-contraction in our simulation is mapped to \textbf{raising $\xi$ past 1}.
\end{examplebox}

\subsection{The two poles}

The denominator $Ms^2+Bs+K=0$ has roots (poles):
\begin{equation}
p_{\pm} = -\omega_n\bigl(\xi \pm \sqrt{\xi^2-1}\bigr)
\label{eq:poles}
\end{equation}

For $\xi>1$ both roots are real and negative. Write:
\begin{align}
p_d &\approx -\frac{K}{B} &&\text{(dominant, \textbf{slow} pole)} \label{eq:pd}\\
p_f &\approx -\frac{B}{M} &&\text{(fast, \textbf{transient} pole)} \label{eq:pf}
\end{align}

\begin{examplebox}{Two poles = two time scales}
After a push, the response is roughly a sum of two decays:
\[
x(t) \approx A_d e^{p_d t} + A_f e^{p_f t}
\]
Because $|p_f|\gg|p_d|$, the fast term $e^{p_f t}$ dies quickly; the slow term
 lingers. That slow term is what you ``feel'' during a normal reach.
\end{examplebox}

\section{Part II --- Time-scale separation and $\rho(\xi)$}
\label{sec:rho}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Separation ratio}

Define the \textbf{separation ratio}:
\begin{equation}
\rho(\xi) = \frac{|p_f|}{|p_d|}
\label{eq:rho_def}
\end{equation}

For $\xi>1$, substituting \eqref{eq:pd}--\eqref{eq:pf} and using
\eqref{eq:omegan}--\eqref{eq:xi}, one obtains the \textbf{closed form} used
everywhere in the code (\texttt{plant.rho}):
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\rho(\xi) = \bigl(\xi + \sqrt{\xi^2-1}\bigr)^2 \quad\text{for }\xi>1,
\qquad \rho(\xi)=1 \text{ for }\xi\le 1\;}
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}

\begin{examplebox}{Numbers for $\rho(\xi)$}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrr@{}}
\toprule
$\xi$ & $\rho(\xi)$ & Plain English \\
\midrule
0.7 & 1.0 & Soft: modes not separated ($\xi<1$ in strict MSD, but simulation uses $\rho=1$). \\
1.2 & 3.5 & Fast mode 3.5$\times$ faster than slow. \\
2.0 & 13.9 & Strong separation. \\
3.0 & 34.0 & Very stiff: transients finish in $\sim$1/34 of the slow time. \\
4.0 & 62.0 & Extreme stiffening. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{examplebox}

\subsection{Singular perturbation: the reduced first-order plant}

When $\rho\gg 1$, the fast mode settles in a time $\tau_f=1/|p_f|$ that is
negligible compared with the movement duration $T$. Standard
\textbf{singular perturbation} replaces the fast mode by its quasi-steady
state and yields a \textbf{first-order} reduced model:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\tau_d \dot x + x = \frac{1}{K}F,
\qquad \tau_d = \frac{B}{K}\;}
\label{eq:reduced}
\end{equation}

\begin{examplebox}{Reduced model}
While very stiff, the coupled system \emph{behaves during one reach} like a
single lag $\tau_d$: push $\Rightarrow$ response ramps up with time constant
$\tau_d$. The fast wobble happened already --- you did not see it. The cup did
not change; your arm \textbf{filtered} the experience.
\end{examplebox}

\section{Part III --- The third-order object in the simulation}
\label{sec:plant}
% =============================================================================

The code does not simulate \eqref{eq:msd} and the object separately on every
time step. It uses a \textbf{phenomenological stand-in} that captures the
\emph{outcome} of coupling: time-scale separation on a fixed object.

\subsection{Object transfer function}

The \textbf{object alone} (the cup) is modelled as three first-order lags in
series:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\; G(s) = \frac{1}{(1+\tau_d s)(1+\tau_1 s)(1+\tau_2 s)}\;}
\label{eq:thirdorder}
\end{equation}

True values in the repository (\texttt{TAU\_TRUE}):
\[
\tau_d = 1.0\,\text{s},\quad \tau_1 = 0.1\,\text{s},\quad \tau_2 = 0.05\,\text{s}
\]
Poles at $s=-1$, $-10$, $-20$\,rad/s.

\begin{examplebox}{Three lags in a row}
Think of three filters in series:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item Slow filter ($\tau_d$): main heaviness.
\item Medium filter ($\tau_1$): first wobble.
\item Fast filter ($\tau_2$): second wobble.
\end{enumerate}
Your command passes through all three; what you feel is the output of the chain.
\end{examplebox}

\subsection{What co-contraction changes}

Co-contraction $\xi$ does \textbf{not} change \eqref{eq:thirdorder}. It changes
what you \textbf{experience} because your arm filters the signal:
\begin{equation}
(\tau_d,\ \tau_1,\ \tau_2)_{\text{felt}}
= \left(\tau_d,\ \frac{\tau_1}{\rho(\xi)},\ \frac{\tau_2}{\rho(\xi)}\right)
\label{eq:felt}
\end{equation}

At $\xi=3$, $\rho\approx34$:
\[
\tau_{1,\text{felt}} \approx 0.003\,\text{s},\quad
\tau_{2,\text{felt}} \approx 0.0015\,\text{s}
\]
During a 0.8\,s reach these are invisible; you feel only $\tau_d=1$\,s.

\subsection{Discrete-time simulation}

The code advances time with step $\dt=0.01$\,s. Each first-order lag with time
constant $\tau$ uses the difference equation (\texttt{cascade\_exact}):
\begin{equation}
y[k] = a\, y[k-1] + (1-a)\, u[k],
\qquad a = e^{-\dt/\tau}
\label{eq:lagdisc}
\end{equation}

Learner~1 uses an older variant (\texttt{cascade}) with one-sample delay per
stage; bias analysis uses \texttt{cascade\_exact} so stiff limiting bias is not
masked by discretisation artefact.

\subsection{Movement and exploration}

Each trial command is:
\begin{equation}
u(t) = u_{\mathrm{ref}}(t) + u_{\mathrm{explore}}(t)
\label{eq:command}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item $u_{\mathrm{ref}}$: smooth out-and-back \textbf{minimum-jerk reach}
  (\texttt{reach\_reference}), duration $t_{\mathrm{move}}$.
\item $u_{\mathrm{explore}}$: \textbf{band-limited noise} (\texttt{band\_limited\_noise}) --- wiggles your arm can actually produce. \emph{Not white noise.}
\end{itemize}

\begin{examplebox}{Why band-limited exploration matters}
White noise contains all frequencies, so it would excite every mode at once.
Then there is no penalty for trying to identify all three lags from trial~1,
and the sample-complexity story collapses. Real reaches are smooth; fast modes
need \textbf{fast movement} to show up.
\end{examplebox}

Felt output with noise:
\begin{equation}
y(t) = \text{cascade}_\text{exact}\bigl(\text{felt}(\tau,\xi), u\bigr) + \text{noise}
\label{eq:output}
\end{equation}

\section{Part IV --- Trial failure (surprise)}
\label{sec:failure}
% =============================================================================

Thrish: ``If stabilisation is not there the ball goes out, the task fails, there
is no learning.'' The code models this as:
\begin{align}
\surprise &= \max_t \bigl|y(t)-\hat y(t)\bigr| \label{eq:surprise}\\
\text{tolerance} &= \mathrm{S\_BASE}\,\frac{\xi}{\xi_{\mathrm{soft}}},
\quad \xi_{\mathrm{soft}}=0.7 \label{eq:tolerance}
\end{align}

If $\surprise>\text{tolerance}$ the trial \textbf{fails} at the first crossing;
only data \textbf{before} failure are used for learning (if enough samples remain).

\begin{examplebox}{Failure}
Soft arm ($\xi=0.7$): tolerance $\approx 0.15$. Small prediction errors fail the trial.

Stiff arm ($\xi=3$): tolerance $\approx 0.64$. Same wrong model may survive.

So stiffness buys \textbf{safety while wrong}, not magic identification speed.
\end{examplebox}

\section{Part V --- The bias law (central finding)}
\label{sec:bias}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Low-frequency expansion}

Expand \eqref{eq:thirdorder} for small $s$ (slow movements):
\begin{equation}
G(s) = \frac{1}{(1+\tau_d s)(1+\tau_1 s)(1+\tau_2 s)}
= 1 - (\tau_d+\tau_1+\tau_2)s + \mathcal O(s^2)
\label{eq:expand}
\end{equation}

A \textbf{first-order learner} uses $\hat G(s)=1/(1+\hat\tau s)=1-\hat\tau s+\cdots$.
Matching the linear term in $s$:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\hat\tau = \tau_d + \tau_1 + \tau_2\;}
\label{eq:bias_soft}
\end{equation}

Bias on the dominant time constant when fitting one lag to a three-lag world:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{bias} = \hat\tau_d - \tau_d \approx \tau_1+\tau_2 = 0.15\,\text{s}
\label{eq:bias_amount}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Effect of co-contraction}

Under stiffening, fast lags in the \emph{felt} signal are divided by $\rho(\xi)$:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
\hat\tau(\xi) = \tau_d + \frac{\tau_1+\tau_2}{\rho(\xi)},
\qquad
\mathrm{bias}(\xi) = \frac{\tau_1+\tau_2}{\rho(\xi)}
\;}
\label{eq:bias_law}
\end{equation}

\begin{resultbox}{Measured in \texttt{bias.py} (12 reaches per $\xi$, least squares)}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
\toprule
$\xi$ & $\rho$ & fitted bias (s) & predicted bias (s) \\
\midrule
0.7 & 1.0 & 0.180 & 0.150 \\
1.2 & 3.5 & 0.040 & 0.043 \\
2.0 & 13.9 & 0.005 & 0.011 \\
3.0 & 34.0 & 0.001 & 0.004 \\
4.0 & 62.0 & 0.0006 & 0.0024 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{resultbox}

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../outputs/learner2/bias_law.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Bias law.} Left: bias in $\hat\tau_d$ vs $\xi$ (data vs
    $(\tau_1+\tau_2)/\rho(\xi)$). Right: fitted $\hat\tau_d$ approaches true
    $\tau_d=1$\,s as $\xi$ increases.}
  \label{fig:bias}
\end{figure}

\begin{examplebox}{Bias law}
You think the cup has one time constant. Soft arm: you measure $\approx1.18$\,s
(sum of all three). Stiff arm ($\xi=4$): you measure $\approx1.0006$\,s, almost
the true $1.0$\,s. Co-contraction did not change the cup --- it stopped the fast
parts from polluting your \textbf{simple} estimate.
\end{examplebox}

\section{Part VI --- Learner 1 (gradient descent, order unlocked by hand)}
\label{sec:learner1}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{What it believes}

Learner~1 (\texttt{learner.py}, \texttt{experiment.py}) always knows the
structure is three lags. It estimates $\hat\tau_d,\hat\tau_1,\hat\tau_2$ but
only updates the first $n_{\mathrm{unlocked}}$ of them; the rest are fixed at
$0$ (pass-through).

Initial guesses: $\hat\tau=(0.30,\,0.03,\,0.015)$ s (wrong on purpose).

\subsection{Prediction}

Given command $u$ and stiffness $\xi$:
\begin{equation}
\hat y(t) = \text{cascade}\bigl(\text{felt}(\hat\tau,\xi), u\bigr)
\label{eq:pred1}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Learning rule}

Keep the last 3 trials. Stack residuals $r = y-\hat y$. Cost
$\mathcal C=\frac12\sum r^2$. Parameters stored as $q=\log\tau$. Each trial,
take 12 steps of gradient descent:
\begin{equation}
q \leftarrow q - \eta\,\frac{J^\top r}{m},
\qquad J_{tk}=\frac{\partial r_t}{\partial q_k}
\label{eq:gd}
\end{equation}
with learning rate $\eta=3$.

\begin{techbox}{Gradient descent in one sentence}
Compute which direction in parameter space would reduce prediction error; take
a fixed-size step that way; repeat. Slow when the problem is
\textbf{ill-conditioned} (some directions barely change the output but look
different in parameter space).
\end{techbox}

\subsection{Conditions S1--S5}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{6.5cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Code} & \textbf{Tuning schedule} & \textbf{What is imposed} \\
\midrule
S1 & soft, fast, 3 modes from start & deep end \\
S2 & $\xi:3\to1.3\to0.7$, slow$\to$fast, unlock $1\to2\to3$ & Thrish curriculum \\
S3 & stiff throughout, 1 mode only & never learns transients (by rule) \\
S4 & soft, speed staged, unlock $1\to2\to3$ & bandwidth channel only \\
S5 & random $\xi$ per trial & control \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Results (20 seeds, 80 trials)}

\begin{warnbox}{Learner~1 refuted the ``curriculum learns faster'' claim}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & median trials to criterion & reached & final error & failed trials \\
\midrule
S1 deep end & 47 & 100\% & 0.043 & 10.4 \\
S2 curriculum & 80 & 55\% & 0.105 & 0.0 \\
S3 stiff forever & --- & 0\% & 0.439 & 0.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
S2 was \textbf{33 trials slower} than S1 ($p=1.6\times10^{-7}$). Curriculum won
on \textbf{safety} (zero failures), not speed.
\end{warnbox}

Two diagnostic facts (\texttt{diagnose.py}):
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item $\kappa(\Phi)$ for the full third-order fit rises from $\sim5.5\times10^3$
  (soft) to $\sim10^{16}$ (stiff): stiffening destroys identifiability of
  transients in the \emph{full} model.
\item Directions that are hardest to learn also change behaviour least (28$\times$
  cheaper in impulse response for 5500$\times$ slower curvature): sample-complexity
  bounds on parameters do not imply behavioural advantage.
\end{enumerate}

    identifiability; ill-conditioned directions are behaviourally cheap; curriculum

\end{figure}

\section{Part VII --- Learner 2 (two-timescale EKF + ARD)}
\label{sec:learner2}
% =============================================================================

Learner~2 fixes two defects of Learner~1:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Misspecification:} Learner~1 always used three lags when unlocked,
  so it could not exhibit the bias law \eqref{eq:bias_law}.
\item \textbf{Uncertainty:} Learner~1 hard-locked modes; Learner~2 must show
  order reduction \emph{emerging} from data, and must not random-walk on
  unobservable modes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{State vector}

\begin{equation}
q = \bigl[\log\hat\tau_d,\ \log\hat\tau_1,\ \log\hat\tau_2,\ w_1,\ w_2\bigr]^\top
\label{eq:state2}
\end{equation}

$w_1,w_2\in[0,1.2]$ are \textbf{participation weights}: how much of each
transient lag is active in the internal model.

\subsection{Blend dynamics (why not just set $\tau=0$?)}

Each transient stage applies:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{blend}(\tau,w)[x] = (1-w)\,x + w\cdot\mathrm{lag}(\tau)[x]
\label{eq:blend}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item $w=1$: full lag with time constant $\tau$.
\item $w=0$: pass-through (mode ``off'' but sensitivity to $w$ remains).
\end{itemize}

Full prediction chain (\texttt{kalman\_learner.\_h}):
\begin{align}
x_0 &= \mathrm{lag}(\hat\tau_d)[u] \nonumber\\
x_1 &= \mathrm{blend}(\hat\tau_1/\rho(\xi), w_1)[x_0] \nonumber\\
\hat y &= \mathrm{blend}(\hat\tau_2/\rho(\xi), w_2)[x_1]
\label{eq:chain2}
\end{align}

\begin{examplebox}{Volume knobs vs deleting a mode}
Wrong approach: ``mode off'' $=$ set $\tau_2=0$. Then the model's output does
not depend on $\tau_2$; the gradient is zero; you can never turn the mode back
on when you relax.

Right approach: ``mode off'' $=$ $w_2=0$. Output unchanged, but $\partial\hat
y/\partial w_2\neq0$. When soft data arrive, $w_2$ can rise again.
\end{examplebox}

\subsection{Effective order}

\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\neff = 1 + w_1 + w_2\;}
\label{eq:neff}
\end{equation}

Roughly: ``how many modes are currently paying for themselves in the model?''
Not imposed --- read from the posterior weights.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) --- full plain-language guide}
\label{sec:ekf}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{techbox}{Kalman filter}
You hold a \textbf{belief} (best guess $\hat q$) and an \textbf{uncertainty}
(covariance $P$). Each trial:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Predict:} allow belief to drift ($P\leftarrow P+Q$).
\item \textbf{Update:} compare prediction to data; move belief toward what
  explains data; shrink uncertainty in directions the data constrain.
\end{enumerate}
If a parameter is not observable this trial, its uncertainty does not shrink
--- you ``know you don't know.''
\end{techbox}

\textbf{Measurement model:} $\hat y = h(q,u,\xi)$ via \eqref{eq:chain2} (nonlinear).

Linearise around current $q$:
\begin{equation}
e = y - h(q),\qquad H = \left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial q}\right|_{q}
\label{eq:lin}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Information-form update} (what the code implements each trial, 3
iterated refinements):
\begin{align}
P_\text{pred}^{-1} &= P^{-1} + Q^{-1} \label{eq:inf_pred}\\
A &= P_\text{pred}^{-1} + \frac{H^\top H}{R} + \diag(\alpha) \label{eq:inf_A}\\
P_\text{post} &= A^{-1} \label{eq:inf_post}\\
q &\leftarrow q + P_\text{post}\left(\frac{H^\top e}{R} - \alpha(q-q_\text{target}) - P_\text{pred}^{-1}(q-q_\text{pred})\right)
\label{eq:inf_update}
\end{align}

Symbols:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item $Q$ --- process noise: how much you allow beliefs to drift between trials
  (small on $\log\hat\tau_d$, larger on transients and weights).
\item $R$ --- measurement variance; inflated to $\max(R_\text{std}^2, \mathrm{mean}(e^2))$
  early so the filter is not overconfident while the model is still wrong.
\item $\alpha$ --- ARD penalties (next section).
\end{itemize}

\begin{examplebox}{EKF}
Day~1: you guess cup time constant 0.3\,s, very unsure ($P$ large). You reach,
feel sluggish response. Filter moves estimate toward 0.8\,s and reduces
uncertainty on $\log\hat\tau_d$. Fast modes stiffened away: columns of $H$ for
$w_1,w_2$ near zero $\Rightarrow$ those uncertainties barely shrink $\Rightarrow$
you do not pretend to have learned them.
\end{examplebox}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{ARD --- Automatic Relevance Determination}
\label{sec:ard}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{techbox}{ARD in plain language}
Each participation weight $w_i$ has a penalty $\alpha_i$ pulling it toward
$0$ (``this wobble probably does not matter''). After each update:
\begin{equation}
\gamma_i = 1 - \alpha_i P_{ii}, \qquad
\alpha_i \leftarrow \frac{\gamma_i}{\max(w_i^2,\varepsilon)}
\label{eq:ard}
\end{equation}
If the data do not support mode~$i$, $P_{ii}$ stays large, $\alpha_i$ stays
moderate, and $w_i$ is shrunk. If soft fast data strongly need mode~$i$,
$w_i$ grows and stays.
\end{techbox}

This is how $\neff$ drops toward 1 while stiff and rises after relaxation
without anyone typing ``unlock mode~2 now.''

\subsection{Two timescales}

Process noise diagonal (rough magnitudes):
\[
Q = \diag(10^{-5},\,2\times10^{-3},\,2\times10^{-3},\,2\times10^{-3},\,2\times10^{-3})
\]
Dominant $\log\hat\tau_d$ drifts slowly (stable memory). Weights and fast
$\tau$'s drift faster (forgetful channel).

\section{Part VIII --- Scoring metrics (every formula)}
\label{sec:metrics}
% =============================================================================

\textbf{Relative impulse error} (how wrong is the learned object model?):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{err} = \frac{\|y_{\mathrm{imp}}-\hat y_{\mathrm{imp}}\|_2}{\|y_{\mathrm{imp}}\|_2}
\label{eq:imperr}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Trials to criterion:} first trial where $\mathrm{err}<0.10$ for 3
consecutive trials.

\textbf{Condition number} (Learner~1 diagnostics):
\begin{equation}
\kappa(\Phi) = \frac{\lambda_{\max}(H^\top H)}{\lambda_{\min}(H^\top H)}
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}

Large $\kappa$: some parameter directions are nearly invisible in the data.

\section{Part IX --- Conclusions and discussion}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{What we conclude}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Co-contraction manufactures time-scale separation} through
  $\rho(\xi)$ \eqref{eq:rho}, matching the mass--spring--damper story
  \eqref{eq:msd}--\eqref{eq:reduced}.

\item \textbf{The precise benefit for a low-order learner is bias reduction}
  \eqref{eq:bias_law}, not generic ``fewer parameters = fewer trials.'' Stiffening
  divides misspecification bias by $\rho$. This is measurable in people.

\item \textbf{Progressive order recruitment can emerge} (Learner~2 K2): $\neff$
  holds near 1 while stiff, rises when co-contraction falls and data expose
  transients.

\item \textbf{Staying stiff forever leaves you at first order} (K3): $\neff=1$,
  unbiased $\hat\tau_d$, plateau error $\approx0.40$ --- discovered, not coded.

\item \textbf{Curriculum reduces risk, not trial count} in these simulations:
  K2/S2 have zero failed trials; deep-end schedules fail more while wrong.

\item \textbf{Humans starting stiff is rational} if the objective is ``do not
  lose trials to catastrophic failure while building a coarse model,'' not
  ``minimise trial count on a correctly specified full-order estimator.''
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What we do \emph{not} conclude}

\begin{warnbox}{Do not claim these in the proposal}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item ``S2/K2 reaches criterion in fewer trials than S1/K1'' --- \textbf{false}
  in both learners for the reported metrics.
\item ``Sample complexity inequality implies faster behavioural learning'' ---
  refuted for Learner~1 (ill-conditioned directions are behaviourally cheap).
\item ``Bandwidth staging alone (K4) is worse than full curriculum'' --- K4 is
  among the fastest schedules in Learner~2.
\item ``Learners always recruit all three modes'' --- $\neff\approx2.2$ because
  the third mode often does not pay for itself under band-limited excitation.
\end{itemize}
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Recommended claim for the URF four-pager}

\begin{quote}
\emph{A learner that treats the coupled system as lower order than it truly is
inherits a bias set by the neglected transients. Co-contraction divides that
bias by the separation ratio $\rho(\xi)$, yielding an unbiased estimate of the
dominant mode while temporarily suppressing effective order. A uncertainty-aware
learner exhibits progressive recruitment when co-contraction relaxes, reaching
the same asymptotic model as an unstiffened learner but with substantially lower
peak error in the dominant mode and fewer failed trials.}
\end{quote}
